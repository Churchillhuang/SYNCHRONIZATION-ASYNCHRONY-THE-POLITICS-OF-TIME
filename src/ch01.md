# PART I: THE CLASSIFICATION PROBLEM {-}


# The Classification Problem


## China: When Labels Stop Working

In contemporary Chinese political discussions, the terms "left" and "right" appear everywhere—in newspapers, academic journals, social media arguments, policy discussions. Government critics accuse policies of being too leftist, while government defenders accuse the critics of being too rightist. Intellectuals carefully position themselves somewhere along this spectrum, and online debates organize around these labels as though everyone knows what they mean.

Ask what they actually mean, though, and things get strange fast.

Consider state intervention in the economy. When market-oriented economists criticize government industrial policy—the state identifying strategic industries, directing investment flows, coordinating research and development—they label it "leftist central planning" that obstructs markets. When egalitarian critics attack the same policy for benefiting large technology companies while ordinary workers see nothing, they call it "rightist state capitalism serving the powerful." The government action doesn't change; the ideological label flips depending on who's complaining and what they're worried about.

Property rights legislation reveals the same pattern. When laws strengthen legal protections for private ownership, critics emphasizing wealth concentration call this "rightist capitalism"—the rich securing their assets against redistribution. Other observers looking at the same laws emphasize protection for small entrepreneurs—the little guy getting legal tools against powerful interests—and label them "leftist." Which classification sticks depends entirely on whose property you're thinking about, not on what the laws actually do.

Market reforms create mirror-image confusion across time. When China first allowed private businesses in the 1980s, critics ferociously attacked this as "rightist capitalism" betraying socialist principles—markets meant exploitation, inequality, bourgeois restoration. Decades later, some observers reversed the charge entirely: expanding markets was actually "leftist" because it abandoned coordinated state planning for chaos. Opening to foreign investment? In the 1980s it was "rightist capitulation to imperialism"—selling out to foreign capital. By the 2000s, critics on a different wavelength called the same policy "leftist cosmopolitanism undermining national industry"—naive internationalism hurting Chinese business. The market opening didn't change; the ideological framing flipped with the decade.

Nationalist positions scatter even more wildly across the spectrum. Strong assertions of sovereignty—standing firm against foreign pressure, defending territorial claims, pursuing independent development paths—get classified as "leftist nationalism" when framed as anti-imperialist resistance to Western domination and capitalist hegemony. The identical sovereignty assertions get classified as "rightist chauvinism" when framed as cultural conservatism defending Chinese civilization and resisting cosmopolitan liberal westernization. The specific policies look the same either way; the difference lies entirely in which external threat you're emphasizing.

Cultural debates follow the same pattern with even more complexity. Traditional values—defending Confucian ethics, preserving family structures, maintaining historical practices—receive opposite classifications depending on which aspect observers emphasize. Focus on hierarchy, social order, and established authority, and these become "rightist conservatism." Focus on collective conformity, suppression of individual freedom, and resistance to modern thinking, and the same traditional values become "leftist feudalism." The practices being defended don't change; what changes is whether you're worried about the hierarchy or the conformity.

Modernization runs into identical labeling chaos. Pursuing social reforms, adopting contemporary practices, and changing established patterns gets labeled "rightist liberalization" when viewed through a market lens—Western capitalism, individual freedom, bourgeois values encroaching on collective solidarity. The same modernization process gets labeled "leftist westernization" when viewed through a cultural lens—foreign influence, abandonment of traditions, cosmopolitan elitism betraying authentic Chinese identity. Whether modernization counts as left or right depends entirely on whether you're thinking economically or culturally.

Education policy reveals how fundamental this confusion becomes. The examination-based meritocracy system that millions of students navigate every year simultaneously embodies "leftist egalitarianism"—anyone can rise through testing regardless of family background—and "rightist elitism"—sorting people into hierarchies based on exam performance. Standardized testing represents "leftist uniformity" because it suppresses individual differences, yet also represents "rightist meritocracy" because it rewards excellence and creates hierarchies. The system operates the same way, produces the same results, but receives opposite ideological labels depending on whether observers focus on the equal-opportunity aspect or the hierarchical-outcome aspect.

Welfare and social services create similar interpretive flexibility. Pension systems, healthcare provision, unemployment insurance—observers label these "leftist redistribution" when emphasizing transfers from productive members to unproductive ones, or "rightist stability maintenance" when emphasizing social control through buying off potential discontent. Both classifications get applied to identical policies. The label that sticks depends on whether you emphasize the redistribution or the social control.

Here's what makes this genuinely puzzling rather than just rhetorical gamesmanship: these aren't random insults people throw around. People engaging in these debates genuinely believe left-right categories are meaningful. Policy disputes organize around them. Positions get defended using this language. Intellectual coalitions form along these supposed lines. Yet the same policy ends up classified both ways.

Try actually mapping Chinese politics onto a left-right spectrum and watch the contradictions multiply. Market reforms don't track consistently one direction. State intervention doesn't correlate with one label. Nationalism appears on both sides simultaneously. Cultural positions refuse to line up neatly. Educational policies scatter. Welfare programs defy placement. It's not that people are using the labels carelessly—they're using them earnestly, but the labels land inconsistently.

This creates a genuine analytical problem. When identical policies receive opposite classifications, when the same position gets called left and right simultaneously, something is fundamentally wrong with the classification scheme.

Now, ask Chinese intellectuals about this confusion and you'll get a consistent answer: "Of course left-right doesn't work properly in China. Our context is different. We have a socialist legacy, Confucian traditions, a unique developmental path. Left-right is a Western framework that doesn't translate to Chinese conditions. The categories mean something different here."

This explanation is comforting. It localizes the problem. Left-right works fine in its original context—it just doesn't export well. Chinese politics is exceptional, so naturally Western categories fail to capture it.

But what if the problem isn't Chinese exceptionalism?


## Philippines: Same Mess, Different System

The Philippines runs on American-style democratic institutions—and not just nominally. Presidential elections every six years where power actually changes hands. Senate and House that actually legislate and argue. Courts that actually rule against the government sometimes. Regular competitive elections where outcomes aren't predetermined. Multiple political parties that actually compete. Media that actually investigates and criticizes. Civil society organizations that actually organize and protest.

The whole Western democratic institutional package is there, transplanted successfully and functioning. Unlike China's single-party system, the Philippines has full multi-party electoral democracy. Regular peaceful transfers of power happen. Opposition parties exist and campaign. Courts exercise judicial review. Press freedom, while sometimes threatened, persists. If left-right categories need Western institutions to work properly, they should work here.

Political discourse certainly uses the language constantly. Parties position themselves as leftist or rightist. Candidates identify as progressive or conservative. Political commentators analyze elections through left-right frameworks. International observers classify Philippine politics using these categories. Academic studies apply the spectrum. Everyone acts like the terms mean something clear and stable.

But watch how politics actually operates, and left-right categories don't organize behavior in any predictable way.

Start with political dynasties—families that hold political offices across generations. The Aquinos, Marcoses, Arroyos, Binays, Estradas—these names appear in Philippine politics for decades. Here's the puzzle: these dynasties move fluidly across the entire supposed left-right spectrum while maintaining continuity in governance approach.

One generation of a family gets labeled "leftist populist"—redistributionist rhetoric, appeals to the poor masses, promises to fight oligarchs, championing land reform, attacking economic elites. The dynasty wins elections using this language. Next generation, same family is suddenly "rightist conservative"—law and order themes dominate, business-friendly policies emerge, emphasis on stability and development, courting investment, maintaining elite relationships.

What actually changed? The family's political base remains stable. The same networks operate—delivering services to supporters, providing local governance, mobilizing voters through personal relationships rather than ideological platforms. The family still represents the same region through similar methods. The governance continuity persists. Only the ideological label shifts.

This pattern appears across multiple families. The puzzle isn't whether family-based politics is good or bad—that's a separate question. The puzzle is: why does left-right classification fail to track what these families actually do? If the classification worked, families should either be consistently leftist or consistently rightist. Instead, the same family receives opposite labels across generations while governance patterns show more continuity than the labels suggest.

Or take another family classified "rightist" because of business connections and wealth. Wealthy family, extensive corporate ties, establishment image—clearly right-wing by traditional metrics. But examine their actual governance and they're implementing extensive welfare programs, expanding social spending, running redistribution schemes that look exactly like what "leftist" parties do elsewhere. Conditional cash transfers to poor families. Universal healthcare expansion. Free education programs. Rice subsidies. Housing assistance.

The classification says "right" but the policies look "left." The puzzle: does the label describe their actual governance, or just their social position?

Or consider a family classified "leftist" because of labor movement roots and worker-friendly rhetoric. Started with union organizing, speaks the language of workers' rights, emphasizes social justice. But observe them in power and they're maintaining cooperative relationships with business interests, preserving established economic arrangements, working within existing power structures. Land reform proceeds slowly. Corporate tax structures remain. Labor regulations develop gradually.

Again, the label and the governance don't map neatly. The puzzle isn't whether this is good governance—reasonable people disagree. The puzzle is: what does "leftist" mean if it doesn't predict policy choices reliably?

Electoral coalitions make the confusion even starker. Election season approaches, and politicians labeled "left" routinely ally with politicians labeled "right" without anyone finding this remotely strange. A "leftist" presidential candidate picks a "rightist" running mate to balance regional support—nobody questions the ideological contradiction because it's transparently about geography and family networks, not ideology.

"Progressive" parties support "conservative" policies once in government to keep coalitions together and maintain power. "Rightist" senators vote consistently with "leftist" representatives on legislation because it serves their overlapping constituencies or family interests. Party switching is common, frequent, and usually has nothing to do with ideological change—it's about tactical positioning, personal conflicts, or family alliances.

These coalitions aren't even stable across time. Bitter allies in one election cycle become fierce opponents in the next. "Left" and "right" politicians migrate between different coalitions election after election without changing any of their stated ideological positions. The musical chairs of alliance formation follows patterns of regional power bases, family networks, and tactical electoral math—not programmatic ideological alignment.

If left-right categories actually organized Philippine politics the way they supposedly do in mature democracies, coalition patterns should follow ideological lines, right? Leftist parties should ally with other leftists to advance shared progressive agendas. Rightist parties with other rightists to advance shared conservative agendas. Cross-ideological coalitions should be rare and uncomfortable compromises.

Instead? Coalition composition bears almost no relationship to left-right positioning. The alliances form and dissolve based on completely different logics.

Economic policy deepens the puzzle. "Leftist" governments privatize state enterprises—selling off government-owned companies to private owners, reducing state economic footprint, embracing market mechanisms. They deregulate markets—removing government controls on business operations, liberalizing trade restrictions, opening sectors to competition. They sign free trade agreements—exposing domestic industries to global competition, reducing tariff protection, integrating into regional trade blocs. All traditionally "rightist" positions according to standard textbook classification.

Meanwhile, "Rightist" governments expand welfare programs—implementing conditional cash transfers to millions of poor families, building public health clinics throughout provinces, funding education expansion, subsidizing rice for low-income consumers, creating employment guarantee schemes. They increase social spending substantially—budget allocations for social services growing faster than GDP, welfare transfers reaching larger portions of population. They implement progressive healthcare schemes approaching universal coverage. All traditionally "leftist" positions.

Both types of government do both types of policies. Sometimes the exact same government does both simultaneously—privatizing telecoms while expanding health coverage, deregulating banking while increasing education spending, signing trade agreements while implementing cash transfers. The classification doesn't predict the policies in any reliable way.

Tax policy creates similar confusion. Progressive taxation—making wealthy people pay higher rates, introducing capital gains taxes, closing corporate loopholes—increases under "rightist" business-oriented administrations because they need revenue for development projects. Regressive consumption taxes—hitting everyone equally regardless of income, expanding VAT coverage, raising fuel taxes—increase under "leftist" redistribution-oriented administrations for the exact same fiscal necessity.

Both implement both approaches based on budget constraints and revenue needs, not ideological commitments to either progressive or regressive taxation principles.

Populist movements prove particularly illuminating for understanding this classificatory confusion. When a candidate emerges mobilizing against elites using fiery anti-establishment rhetoric, promising to drain the swamp, fight corruption, and restore power to "the people"—something that happens regularly in Philippine politics—observers disagree fundamentally about whether this represents left-wing or right-wing populism.

Those emphasizing opposition to oligarchs and economic inequality classify it as "left-wing populism"—fighting the wealthy elite, representing the poor masses, challenging concentrated economic power structures, promising redistribution of resources. Those emphasizing opposition to liberal elites and westernized cosmopolitanism classify the same movement as "right-wing populism"—fighting the cosmopolitan establishment, representing traditional Filipino values, challenging foreign cultural influence, resisting progressive social change.

Yet the movement's actual content hasn't changed between these classifications. The same anti-elite mobilization, the same nationalist appeals, the same anti-corruption promises, the same pledges to fight for ordinary people against powerful interests—all remain constant. Only the classification varies depending on which aspect external observers choose to emphasize when forcing the movement into left-right categories that don't actually fit the phenomenon they're trying to describe.

Regional politics adds yet another layer of classification confusion. National parties have clear left-right labels—everyone knows which parties are "left" and which are "right" at the national level. But local politicians affiliated with these national parties often govern differently than the national label would suggest.

A local politician affiliated with a national "leftist" party might govern quite traditionally at the local level—maintaining established social structures, working within traditional authority patterns, preserving regional customs, building on existing local relationships. The national leftist label and the locally rooted governance coexist without apparent contradiction.

The reverse happens just as frequently. Politicians affiliated with "rightist" national parties implement local reforms—expanding social services to previously underserved communities, pursuing land redistribution, supporting cooperative organizations, challenging some established local interests. Progressive local governance under a conservative national party label.

Regional political leaders shift between "left" and "right" national parties from election to election while their local governance approach remains relatively consistent. The national classification appears tactical and flexible. The local political practice shows more continuity.

Now here's why all this matters for our puzzle about left-right classification. The Philippines has functional Western democratic institutions—not aspirational institutions existing only on paper, not facade institutions covering authoritarian reality, but actually functional democratic structures. The presidential system modeled directly on the United States actually works, with competitive elections, peaceful power transfers, and presidents leaving office when terms end. Electoral outcomes aren't predetermined—opposition parties win regularly, incumbent parties lose, uncertainty remains real. The party system is genuinely fragmented, coalitions matter, small parties exercise influence. Despite intimidation attempts, investigative journalism persists, scandals get exposed, corruption gets reported. The supreme court rules against presidents, judicial review operates, legal constraints function. NGOs organize, protests happen, grassroots movements mobilize.

These institutions aren't perfect. Corruption exists. Violence occurs. Democratic quality varies. But the basic institutional structures of Western liberal democracy are present and functioning at a reasonable level.

If left-right classification requires these institutions to function properly, if it needs Western political forms to stabilize, if democratic institutions are supposed to make the categories work... then the Philippines should be clear. Western institutions should make left-right categories organize political behavior predictably.

They don't. Despite functional Western democratic institutions, left-right classification still drifts unpredictably, reverses unexpectedly, and fails to organize political behavior consistently. Electoral democracy operates robustly, but left-right categories don't structure political competition. Parties span the supposed ideological spectrum, but coalition patterns completely ignore these ideological positions. Competitive elections happen regularly, but left-right positioning doesn't predict who allies with whom, what policies get implemented, or how governance actually operates.

The pattern we saw in China appears in the Philippines despite completely different political systems. Same policies get opposite labels depending on context. Same politicians move across the spectrum depending on circumstances. Same rhetorical categories fail to stabilize into consistent meanings. Same analytical confusion results.

But—and this is crucial for understanding that the problem isn't institutional—China isn't a multi-party democracy. China doesn't have competitive presidential elections where outcomes are uncertain. China's courts don't independently constrain government. China's media isn't free to investigate and criticize power. China's civil society isn't free to organize opposition.

If these institutional differences explained Chinese labeling confusion, then the Philippines should be clear. Western institutions should make left-right categories work properly. Democratic competition should force ideological coherence. Electoral accountability should stabilize positions. Institutional constraints should organize political behavior along ideological lines.

None of this happens. The institutions function, but the categories still float freely.

Ask Filipino political scientists about this and you'll hear something familiar: "Well, Philippine democracy is still developing its own form. We have strong family networks in politics, regional identities, personal relationship-based governance. Left-right is a framework that developed in Western contexts with different social structures and political histories. Philippine politics operates through different mechanisms. The categories don't apply the same way here."

Again, the explanation localizes the problem. Left-right works fine in its home context—it just hasn't fully mapped onto societies with different political organization. Philippine distinctiveness explains the confusion.

But notice what just happened. We've now heard two "exceptional" explanations from two very different political systems:

China says: "We're special because we're not Western democracies—we have socialist legacy, single-party rule, different developmental path."

Philippines says: "We're special because we have different social organization—we have family networks in politics, regional structures, relationship-based governance."

Both explain the confusion by claiming local distinctiveness. Both assume left-right works fine elsewhere—just not here, for our particular reasons.

They can't both be right about why the categories fail. Unless...


## The West: Wait, It Happens There Too?

The obvious escape route: maybe left-right only confuses when applied outside its Western origin? Chinese exceptionalism and Philippine immaturity both assume the categories work fine where they came from—in established Western democracies with long liberal traditions and programmatic party systems.

Surely there, in the birthplace of left-right classification, the categories remain stable?

Turn to Western political analysis and you find... exactly the same localization move. European scholars explain American political confusion: "Americans don't have real left-right politics—they lack a genuine left party, they're all liberal individualists, their politics is about personalities not ideologies." American scholars explain European confusion: "European politics is fragmented by proportional representation, multiple parties, coalition governments—not like our clear two-party system." Each Western context claims its neighbor misuses the categories.

But set aside these mutual accusations and look at the actual pattern. Within Western politics itself—regardless of which country—left-right classifications have reversed repeatedly over time.

### Free Speech and Expression

In the 1960s and 70s, the pattern seemed clear enough. The political left defended free speech against authority—student movements fought for uncensored expression on campuses, civil rights advocates opposed government censorship, anti-war activists claimed the right to controversial speech. The Free Speech Movement at Berkeley became definitively coded as a left-wing cause, part of a broader progressive challenge to established authority.

Fast forward to contemporary debates, and the pattern has substantially reversed. Left positions now frequently support restricting speech through content moderation on platforms, deplatforming controversial speakers, limiting "harmful" expression, and implementing speech codes on campuses. Meanwhile right positions increasingly invoke free speech defenses, protesting deplatforming decisions, opposing content moderation policies, fighting speech codes, and defending controversial expression against censorship.

The specific content differs, of course—1960s leftists defended political dissent against Cold War repression while contemporary rightists defend cultural transgression against progressive norms. But structurally, the positions on speech regulation have swapped sides. The historical left opposed authorities limiting speech in the name of national security or public morals. The contemporary left supports authorities limiting speech in the name of preventing harm or protecting vulnerable groups. The reversal is real and substantial.

Both sides claim consistency in their principles. Progressive advocates argue that harmful speech causes material damage requiring regulation—this isn't censorship in the traditional sense but protection of vulnerable communities from assault. Conservative advocates argue that free expression requires defending against whoever currently holds institutional power to censor—this isn't a new position but continued resistance to authority, just with different authorities. Each camp sees itself as maintaining principled commitments. Yet their respective stances on speech regulation have clearly reversed their left-right classifications.

### Emergency Powers

After September 11, 2001, debates over expanded state power carried clear ideological valence. Expanded surveillance for security purposes, detention without trial as emergency measures, increased executive authority for crisis response—these positions were predominantly classified as "right-wing" stances emphasizing security over liberty. Critics who opposed these expansions came predominantly from the left—civil liberties advocates, progressive organizations, anti-war movements warning against sacrificing freedom for security.

Jump forward to 2020-2021 and the COVID-19 pandemic, and the classifications had substantially shifted. Lockdown powers for public health necessity, vaccine mandates for collective responsibility, executive emergency orders justified by scientific imperatives—these positions were now predominantly classified as "left-wing" stances emphasizing collective welfare over individual liberty. Critics who opposed these expansions came predominantly from the right—libertarians, conservatives, anti-mandate movements warning against sacrificing freedom for security.

The structural pattern connecting both cases is nearly identical: perceived emergency justifies expanded state power, critics worry about the liberty-versus-security tradeoff, debate centers on whether circumstances truly warrant exceptional authority. What flipped between the two cases was the ideological classification attached to each position. Emergency surveillance after terrorism received right-wing coding, while emergency mandates during pandemic received left-wing coding, despite the actual mechanisms of state power expansion looking remarkably similar across both scenarios.

### Economic Policy Puzzles

Nordic social democracies—Sweden, Denmark, Norway—are consistently classified as "left" or "center-left" in political analysis, and the reasoning seems straightforward enough: high taxes, extensive welfare states, strong social safety nets, universal healthcare, generous unemployment benefits, with government spending reaching 50% of GDP. This certainly looks like leftist economic policy by traditional criteria.

Look closer at how these economies actually function, though, and complications emerge. Markets allocate resources extensively while private ownership dominates across sectors. Businesses operate with relatively light regulation compared to many economies classified as more "rightist." Trade flows freely, capital moves openly, entrepreneurship thrives, and property rights remain strong. By traditional right-wing economic criteria, these are highly market-oriented economies that depend fundamentally on private enterprise and competitive markets.

The classification system struggles to handle economies that combine extensive state spending with robust market mechanisms. It assumes these must trade off against each other—more market should mean less state, more state should mean less market. But Nordic systems demonstrate they can coexist, which raises the question: are we classifying based on redistribution or market mechanisms? And if both matter, what happens when they point in opposite directions?

Chile under Pinochet created the opposite puzzle from a different angle. The regime maintained military authoritarianism—arresting opponents, censoring media, concentrating power in the executive—which political criteria clearly mark as authoritarian right-wing governance. Economically, however, it implemented radical free-market reforms under the guidance of the "Chicago Boys" advisors: massive privatization, sweeping deregulation, trade liberalization, reduced state spending, floating currency. By economic criteria, this represented extreme right-wing capitalism in its purest doctrinal form.

The puzzle deepens when considering what this implies. If right-wing governance means both authoritarian politics and free markets, where do democratic societies with free markets fit? Are they only partially right-wing? The category seems to assume that authoritarian politics and market economics naturally align, but democratic market economies exist alongside authoritarian market economies. The assumed link turns out not to be necessary at all.

Contemporary China multiplies the confusion exponentially. The Communist Party maintains complete political monopoly—single-party rule, no opposition parties, censored media, controlled elections—which political criteria straightforwardly identify as left-authoritarian governance. Economically, however, private enterprises operate extensively across most sectors, markets allocate the majority of resources, entrepreneurship receives active celebration, billionaires accumulate substantial wealth, stock markets function actively, and international trade reaches massive scales. By economic criteria, this increasingly resembles capitalist market organization.

Traditional classification simply breaks down when confronting this combination. Calling it "left" requires ignoring the market economy that actually organizes most economic activity. Calling it "right" requires ignoring the Communist Party that maintains complete political control. Calling it "center" or "mixed" makes no sense—there's nothing moderate or compromising about either the political monopoly or the market dynamism. Each operates at full strength in its respective domain.

### Trade and Globalization

Through the 1990s and early 2000s, positions on international trade seemed relatively stable. The left opposed free trade agreements through labor unions fighting NAFTA, progressive activists protesting the WTO, and anti-globalization movements mobilizing from left-wing bases. Opposition centered on protecting workers from global wage competition, preventing regulatory races to the bottom, and preserving national labor standards against erosion from international economic integration.

The right supported trade liberalization through conservative governments signing agreements, business groups pushing for open markets, and free trade advocacy emerging from right-aligned think tanks and policy circles. Support emphasized economic efficiency through comparative advantage, benefits from market expansion, and wealth creation through international exchange.

By the 2010s, these positions had substantially scrambled. The right increasingly opposed trade agreements through nationalist movements resisting globalization, conservative politicians withdrawing from international institutions, and populist right-wing movements attacking free trade orthodoxy. Opposition now centered on protecting national jobs from foreign competition, preserving sovereignty against international organizations, and resisting what was framed as economic threats to national communities.

The left increasingly defended international economic cooperation through progressive movements supporting maintained trade relationships, liberal politicians advocating for global institutions, and cosmopolitan left-wing voices emphasizing international coordination. Support now centered on climate cooperation requiring international frameworks, international solidarity transcending national boundaries, and cosmopolitan values requiring global engagement.

The underlying question—how much economic integration versus how much national autonomy?—remained constant across both periods. What reversed was which ideological camp occupied which position. Labor protection arguments migrated from left to right. Market efficiency arguments migrated from right to left. The reversals occurred not because the issues changed, but because the political coalitions supporting each position reorganized themselves along different lines.

### Immigration

Historically, left positions emphasized international worker solidarity transcending national borders. Socialist movements advocated for open migration on the principle that workers have no country, while labor internationalism opposed border controls as artificially dividing the working class. This constituted the traditional left position on immigration—borders as capitalist constructs dividing workers who share common interests.

Right positions emphasized border control and national limits to immigration. Conservative movements advocated restrictive immigration policies to protect national identity and cultural cohesion. Business interests sometimes created internal tensions by wanting immigrant labor for economic reasons, but cultural conservatism generally aligned with restriction and maintained immigration limits.

Contemporary patterns have partially reversed these traditional alignments. Significant left constituencies now support enforcement through workplace verification, deportation policies, and border security—framing these as protecting domestic workers from wage competition and preserving hard-won labor standards. Some unions oppose immigration expansion as threatening jobs and worker bargaining power. Some progressive voices question whether unlimited immigration remains compatible with maintaining robust welfare states that depend on bounded communities of mutual obligation.

Meanwhile, significant right constituencies now oppose strict enforcement. Business interests advocate expanded immigration to ensure adequate labor supply across economic sectors. Libertarian voices oppose state power to restrict movement as violating individual liberty. Some conservatives emphasize immigrant economic contributions to growth and dynamism, questioning restrictionist policies that might slow economic development.

The debate now cuts across left-right classification rather than following it. Border enforcement becomes simultaneously leftist worker protection and rightist xenophobia depending on interpretive frame. Immigration expansion becomes simultaneously rightist labor supply and leftist cosmopolitanism depending on which aspect observers emphasize. The same policies receive opposite classifications based on which justification or consequence gets foregrounded in political argument.

### What This Reveals

These aren't cherry-picked anomalies. Systematic patterns appear across multiple Western democracies and policy domains. Speech regulation, emergency powers, trade policy, immigration—in all these areas, positions coded "left" in one period get coded "right" in another, and vice versa.

The specific issues vary, but the phenomenon repeats consistently: classifications reverse while underlying questions persist. If left-right categories worked reliably within their Western origin context, similar positions should maintain similar classifications over time, but they demonstrably don't maintain such stability.

Here's the revealing part: ask Western political scientists about this instability and you'll hear yet another localization move—temporal rather than spatial this time. Some argue that politics has fundamentally changed—the old left-right axis organized around economics (state versus market, labor versus capital), which made sense, but now politics centers on culture, identity, and values, so the categories can't fit these new issues without updating the framework. Others insist we're experiencing a realignment period where party coalitions are shifting and traditional alignments are breaking down—once things stabilize into new coalitions, left-right categories will make sense again, making the confusion merely temporary. Still others blame populism for scrambling everything—the real divide has become populist versus elite rather than left versus right, and once this populist moment passes, normal politics will return.

Notice the pattern connecting all these explanations—Chinese, Filipino, and Western. China explains its confusion by pointing to socialist legacy, Confucian traditions, and single-party rule. The Philippines explains its confusion by pointing to family networks in politics, regional structures, and relationship-based governance. Western scholars explain their confusion by pointing to cultural issues, realignment periods, and populist disruption.

Everyone localizes the problem through the same move: "The categories work fine in general, just not here or now because of our special circumstances." Each context claims unique reasons explaining why a supposedly universal framework doesn't apply universally in their case.

But if the categories fail in China and the Philippines and the United States and Europe, if they fail during economic politics and cultural politics, if they flip over time within the same countries using the same institutions, if they've been "temporarily confused" for decades...

Then maybe the problem isn't local. Maybe it's not about immature democracies or special cultures or new issues or realignment periods.

Maybe the problem is the categories themselves.

What if left-right classification creates instability everywhere because it's classifying the wrong thing?


## What Actually Divides Politics?

Here's the core problem we've uncovered: left-right categories classify policies—state intervention versus markets, change versus tradition, equality versus hierarchy. But identical policies can serve fundamentally different purposes.

State intervention can preserve existing order or transform it completely. Markets can enable gradual evolution or disrupt traditional communities. The policy itself doesn't reveal what it's for.

This explains the instability. When categories attach to policies rather than purposes, identical policies serving different purposes get different labels. When purposes shift but policies stay similar, classifications flip. The categories track means rather than ends.

So what if we classified based on something deeper—not what policies are chosen, but why they're chosen? Not surface tools, but underlying purposes?

Traditional criteria fail because they operate at the wrong level. They catalog policies without examining premises. They describe means without probing ends.

A valid criterion would need to reach beneath policies to the fundamental assumptions that generate them. It would need to explain both why certain positions cluster together (Burke and Hayek despite different contents) and why opposite ideologies sometimes produce similar patterns (authoritarian regimes with opposite rhetoric employing similar control mechanisms).

What kind of criterion could do this? What fundamental question precedes all political choices—determining not just what policies are selected, but what purposes those policies serve?

This question awaits an answer. What follows will provide it.
